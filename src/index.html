Seven warning signs before a market bubble pops

A bubble is when prices climb far above what the underlying thing is actually worth. These indicators have flashed before most of the big crashes in history.

Master graph: SPY & QQQ vs the 7 warning signs

When the warning lights were on, what did the market do next?

How to read it

Top: SPY (the S&P 500) and QQQ (the Nasdaq-100, mostly big tech) on a log scale, so a 50% drop looks the same size in 1987 as in 2022. Red bands run from each crash's peak to its bottom, with a dot at the peak.

Bottom rows: one row per warning sign, one cell per year. Red is flashing, amber is elevated, gray is normal. Faint red bars behind the prices count how many signs were flashing. Use the chips to show or hide signs, and hover or tap any year for details.

See the master graph on the left.

What it's telling you

The dot-com crash had almost everything flashing. By 2000, six of the seven signs were red: extreme valuations, rate hikes, an inverted yield curve, and record margin debt. QQQ then fell 83%.

2008 came from a different place. Only two signs were flashing in 2007. Stocks weren't wildly expensive; the danger was in housing debt and the banks, which these seven gauges only partly capture.

Some crashes come from outside. Black Monday (1987) and COVID (2020) hit with few signs red. No valuation gauge predicts a pandemic or a one-day panic.

Today: four signs are flashing in 2026 (CAPE, ECY, Buffett indicator, margin debt) and two are elevated (rate hikes, breadth). Only the yield curve reads normal. 2025 had five signs flashing, the most since 2000's six.

How the colors are set: CAPE flashes at 35%+ above its era's normal (≈15 before 1990, ≈27 after 2002) and is elevated at 15%+. ECY flashes below 2% and is elevated below 3%. The Buffett indicator flashes at 140%+ of GDP and is elevated at 110%+. The yield curve flashes when inverted and is elevated when nearly flat. Rate hikes flash during hiking cycles of 1.5+ points. Breadth and margin debt are judgment calls based on how the typical stock did vs the index, and how fast margin debt grew vs the market.

Fact check: should today's CAPE be judged against 22–25?

Partly right. Today's giants are asset-light and high-margin, and accounting changes in the 1990s push reported earnings down in bad years, which inflates CAPE (Jeremy Siegel's critique). So a higher "normal" than the old average of about 17 is a fair argument. Since 1990, CAPE has averaged about 27, so 22–25 is, if anything, conservative.

But the conclusion holds. At 41.4, CAPE is 66–88% above 22–25, and about 51% above the post-1990 average, which itself includes the dot-com bubble. Applying the same fixes to history lowers the old average too. And record profit margins have tended to fall back, which would mean today's CAPE understates the risk.

A likely mix-up: if Gemini meant today's CAPE "really" is 22–25 after adjusting, no standard adjustment gets there. The number near 22 is the forward P/E (price ÷ next year's expected earnings), a different and more optimistic measure.

What I adjusted: Graphs 1, 2, 3, and 6 have a "Tech-adjusted" view. Graphs 4, 5, and 7 don't need one, and each of those sections explains why.

Act 1 · Valuation

Graph 1: Shiller CAPE vs regular P/E

Are stocks expensive compared to the profits companies actually make?

The formula

P/E=PriceLast 12 months of earnings
CAPE=PriceAverage of the last 10 years of earnings, adjusted for inflation

Example (Oct 2, 2026): CAPE = 41.4, so investors pay $41.40 for every $1 of average inflation-adjusted profit. Regular P/E = 26.3.

▲ HigherMore expensive: you pay more for each $1 of profit. CAPE above 30 came before the biggest crashes.

▼ LowerCheaper. Normal CAPE was about 15 before the 1990s and about 27 since. Readings near 7–10 (1932, 1982) were great times to buy.

What they measure

Both lines show how many dollars investors pay for $1 of S&P 500 profit. If you buy a store that earns $100k a year for $1.7M, you're paying 17× its profit.

Regular P/E (gray dashed line) uses only the last 12 months of profit. It's the number quoted in the news.

Shiller CAPE (blue line) uses the 10-year average profit, adjusted for inflation. Nobel laureate Robert Shiller created it to smooth out boom and bust years.

How to read the graph

Higher means more expensive. The dotted lines show "normal." They step up in the 1990s, as accounting changes and high-margin tech companies lifted typical valuations: CAPE goes from about 15 to about 27, and P/E from about 14 to about 21. Each dot is a peak that was followed by a drop, and the size of the drop is written above it. Red dots are the three famous bubble crashes, and amber dots are the other drops. The buttons show one line at a time. Hover or tap a point for details.

See Graph 1 on the left.

What it's telling you

Extreme CAPE readings came before the worst crashes. CAPE was about 44 in 1999, more than double normal, and the market then fell 49%. It was 33 in 1929, and the market then lost 86%.

A crash doesn't need a sky-high CAPE. In 1906 and 1937, CAPE was only about 21–22, yet stocks fell about half: a bank panic followed in 1907, and money was tightened too early in 1937. After 1966, the market fell 22% and then went nowhere for 16 years while inflation ate away at it. That slow grind is the other way an overpriced market corrects.

A low CAPE means opportunity. The 1982 low kicked off an 18-year rally.

Regular P/E gives false alarms. When profits collapse, it spikes: about 70 in 2009 (one of the best times to buy in decades), about 46 in 2002, and about 36 in 2021. It also misses real danger. In 1929 it was only about 20, because boom-time profits were high, while CAPE showed 33.

The takeaway: regular P/E is like valuing a store off one month of sales. CAPE values it off a 10-year record, so it's the better bubble gauge. A high CAPE tells you how much there is to lose, not when the fall will happen. Today's reading of about 41 is above the 1929 and 2021 peaks and second only to dot-com.

Tech-era adjustment

Use the "Tech-adjusted" button to compare today's CAPE with three ideas of "normal." Even against the most generous one, the post-1990 average (27.4), today's reading is about 50% higher. Fast tech profit growth makes the 10-year average lag, which is partly why P/E (26) is so far below CAPE (41). But 26 is still well above P/E's tech-era normal of about 21.

Latest: the last 12 months

CAPE rose from about 39 to 41.4 (October 2, 2026), with a dip to 37 in March. That's above the 1929 peak (32.6) and the 2021 peak (38.6). Only the dot-com peak (44.2) was higher. Regular P/E eased from about 28 to 26 because profits grew faster than prices. Recent profits are strong, but prices are high compared with the 10-year profit record.

Act 1 · Valuation

Graph 2: Excess CAPE Yield (ECY)

Are stocks paying enough extra to be worth the risk over safe bonds?

The formula

Earnings yield=1CAPE
Real bond yield=10-year Treasury yield − Inflation
ECY=Earnings yield − Real bond yield

Example (Oct 2, 2026): 1 ÷ 41.4 = 2.42%. 10-year TIPS real yield = 2.92%. ECY = 2.42% − 2.92% = −0.50%.

▲ HigherCheaper and better for stocks: a big extra reward over safe bonds (13% in 1932, 10% in 1982).

▼ LowerRiskier. Below 2% is the danger zone. Below 0, safe bonds are expected to beat stocks (1929, 1999). This is the opposite direction from Graph 1.

What it measures

CAPE ignores interest rates. A CAPE of 30 means something very different when safe bonds pay 5% than when they pay 1%. Shiller's ECY (2020) accounts for this in two steps.

1. Flip CAPE upside down. 1 ÷ CAPE is the "earnings yield," the profit you get per dollar invested. A CAPE of 25 means a 4% earnings yield, like a $500k rental property that nets $20k a year.

2. Subtract what a safe bond pays after inflation (the 10-year Treasury rate minus inflation). What's left is the extra return for taking on stock risk. If stocks yield 4% and bonds pay 2%, the ECY is 2%.

How to read the graph

Higher is cheaper, lower is riskier, the opposite of Graph 1. The red band at the bottom is the danger zone, below 2%. Below the 0 line, a safe bond is expected to beat stocks. The dots mark the same peaks as Graph 1.

See Graph 2 on the left.

What it's telling you

Bubbles: ECY was below zero in 1929 and 1999. Safe bonds were expected to beat stocks, yet people kept buying stocks. It was also low, about 1.7%, in 2007.

Buying moments: about 13% in 1932, 10% in 1982, and 8% in 2009. The years that followed were excellent.

What ECY adds that CAPE can't: in 2020–21, CAPE was 38, but ECY stayed around 3–5% because bonds paid almost nothing after inflation. Then the 2022 rate hikes (Graph 5) shrank that cushion, and the market fell 25%.

Where it stands now: about 1.0% on Shiller's own measure in September 2026, the thinnest cushion since 2002. Stocks are very expensive and bonds pay a solid real return, so stocks offer very little extra reward for their risk.

Tech-era adjustment

ECY already adjusts CAPE for interest rates. The "Tech-adjusted" view uses more generous earnings measures for fast-growing companies. With the last 12 months of earnings, the cushion is about 0.9%. With analysts' forecasts for next year, it's about 1.6%. Even that is about half the historical median of roughly 3%.

Latest: the last 12 months

The 12-month graph uses the inflation-protected (TIPS) bond yield, which reads a bit lower than Shiller's 1.0%. It slid from about 0.8% last October to roughly zero by September, then dipped below zero on October 2 when the 10-year Treasury yield jumped to 5.28%. A safe inflation-protected bond now pays about as much as stocks' long-run earnings yield, or more. That last happened in 1929 and 1999–2000. It still doesn't tell you when anything will happen, and a one-day reading can reverse.

Act 1 · Valuation

Graph 3: Buffett indicator

Is the stock market worth more than the economy that produces its profits?

The formula

Buffett indicator=Total US stock market valueUS GDP×100%
Tech-adjusted=Total US stock market valueAfter-tax corporate profits

Example: $2.32 of market value for every $1 of GDP = 232%, the May 2026 record. Tech-adjusted, Q1 2026: about 20.2× profits.

▲ HigherThe market is worth more compared with the economy. Above 150% has meant prices were far ahead of reality.

▼ LowerCheaper. 100% is roughly fair, and 35% (1982) was a generational low.

What it measures

This divides the value of every US public company by GDP, which is one year of everything the US economy produces. Companies earn their money from the economy, so a market worth far more than the economy suggests prices have gotten ahead of reality. It's Warren Buffett's favorite big-picture valuation gauge.

How to read the graph

The dashed line at 100% is roughly fair: the market is worth one year of economic output. Above the line is expensive, and below it is cheap. Above 150% has historically meant prices were badly disconnected from the economy.

See Graph 3 on the left.

What it's telling you

1982: about 35%, very cheap. 2000: about 146%, then roughly halved by 2002 in the dot-com crash. 2007: only about 105%, yet the 2008 crash still came, because it was driven by housing debt rather than stock prices. 2021: about 200%, then the 2022 drop. The right edge is climbing back toward that peak.

The caveat: the whole line has drifted upward over 50 years. US companies now earn a lot overseas, which doesn't count in US GDP, and they're more profitable than in the 1970s. So "normal" today is likely above 100%. Use this indicator to compare against past peaks, not as an exact threshold.

Tech-era adjustment

This indicator gets the biggest adjustment. Dividing market value by corporate profits instead of GDP removes the overseas-profit distortion. That gives about 20× profits in early 2026, vs about 30.5× at the dot-com peak. On this measure, the market is expensive but not at a record. The catch is that it assumes today's unusually high profits hold up.

Latest: the last 12 months

It kept setting records: about 220% at the end of September 2025, about 232% in May 2026, and roughly 237% in September 2026 by one widely quoted measure. Data providers calculate it differently, so figures range from about 217% to 250%, but they all put it above both the 2021 peak and the dot-com bubble.

Act 2 · Money and interest rates

Graph 4: Yield curve inversion

What are bond investors predicting about the economy?

The formula

Yield spread=10-year Treasury yield − 2-year Treasury yield
Invertedwhen the spread is below 0

Example (Oct 2, 2026): spread = +0.45 percentage points, so the curve is not inverted.

▲ HigherNormal: long-term loans pay more than short-term ones. This is not a recession warning.

▼ LowerBelow 0 the curve is inverted, meaning investors expect a slowdown. Recessions have usually followed within 12–24 months.

What it measures

Normally, lending to the US government for 10 years pays more interest than lending for 2 years, because your money is locked up longer. This graph shows the 10-year rate minus the 2-year rate.

When the 2-year pays more, the curve is inverted. That usually means the Fed has pushed short-term rates way up, and investors expect the economy to weaken enough that rates will have to fall later.

How to read the graph

Above zero (blue) is normal. Below zero (red) is inverted, which is the warning. Gray bars are recessions. Look for a red dip followed by a gray bar a year or two later.

See Graph 4 on the left.

What it's telling you

  1. Red dip around 1978–80, then recessions in 1980–82.
  2. Small red dip in 1989, then a recession in 1990–91.
  3. Red dip in 2000, then a recession in 2001.
  4. Red dip in 2006–07, then the 2008–09 financial crisis.
  5. A brief touch of zero in 2019, then the 2020 recession.

Why it works: banks borrow short-term and lend long-term. When short rates are higher than long rates, lending stops paying off, so credit dries up and the economy slows. The delay between inversion and recession has usually been 12 to 24 months.

The big exception: 2022–24 was the deepest and longest inversion in decades, and no recession has followed so far. That's either the indicator's biggest miss or a very long delay. Treat it as a smoke alarm, not a countdown clock.

Tech-era adjustment

No adjustment needed. The yield curve measures the cost and availability of money, which affects tech and non-tech companies alike.

Latest: the last 12 months

The curve is not inverted right now. The spread was about +0.55 in early October 2025, about +0.38 in July 2026, and +0.45 on October 2, 2026. The 2-year yield rose about a third, to 4.78%, as markets priced in Fed hikes, and the 10-year jumped to 5.28%. One caution: recessions have often started after the curve turned positive again, so a positive curve isn't an all-clear on its own.

Act 2 · Money and interest rates

Graph 5: Central bank rate hikes

What actually pops the bubble?

The formula

Size of a hiking cycle=Peak policy rate − Rate before hikes began

Example (2022–23): 5.50% − 0.25% = 5.25 percentage points. 2026 so far: 4.00% − 3.75% = 0.25 points.

▲ HigherCheap money disappears fast. Every major bubble on this list ended after big hikes.

▼ LowerBorrowing stays cheap, which keeps bubbles inflating.

What it measures

Central banks such as the Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan set the base interest rate for the whole economy. Low rates make borrowing cheap, so people and companies borrow to buy stocks and houses and to grow businesses. Bubbles grow on cheap money. Rate hikes, usually meant to fight inflation, take it away.

How to read the graph

Each pair of bars is one hiking cycle. The gray bar is the rate before hikes began, and the orange bar is the peak. What happened next is written below each pair. A bigger gap means borrowing got expensive faster.

See Graph 5 on the left.

What it's telling you

Every major bubble on this list ended after a period of rate hikes. The most dramatic were 1% → 5.25% before the housing crash and 0.25% → 5.5% before the 2022 bear market, a move from nearly free money to expensive money.

If your credit line goes from 1% to 5.5%, you stop borrowing to grow, and so does everyone else. And when a savings account pays 5%, paying 40× profit for risky stocks looks worse, so money leaves the market.

How it connects to Graph 4: hikes push short-term rates up fast while long-term rates lag behind. That's usually what inverts the yield curve.

Tech-era adjustment

No adjustment needed. Rate hikes raise borrowing costs for everyone. If anything, tech giants are more sensitive to rates, because more of their value depends on profits expected far in the future.

Latest: the last 12 months

This is the biggest change of the last 12 months. The Fed cut rates in October and December 2025 to 3.50–3.75%, then held for nine months. On September 16, 2026, it hiked to 3.75–4.00%, its first hike since July 2023, because inflation stayed above target. Its projections point to about one more hike by the end of 2026. The pin is being picked up again, though 4% is still below the peak rates before past crashes.

Act 3 · Market structure

Graph 6: Narrow leadership

Is the whole market rising, or just a few giant companies?

The formula

A stock's weight=Its market valueTotal market value of all 500
Breadth gap=Equal-weight index return − Regular (cap-weighted) index return

Example (Sep 2026): the 10 biggest stocks are 37.8% of the index. Over 3 years, the breadth gap was −16.3%.

▲ HigherA positive gap means the typical stock is keeping up: a broad, healthy rally.

▼ LowerA negative gap means a few giants are carrying the index while most stocks lag. That gets fragile once the leaders stall.

What it measures

The S&P 500 is weighted by company size, so the biggest companies count far more. A handful of giants can lift the index even while most stocks are falling. "Breadth" asks how many stocks are actually taking part in the rally.

How to read the graph

This is a simplified illustration of the pattern, not real data. Both lines start at 100. The solid blue line is the index you hear about in the news, and the dashed gray line is the typical stock. Read it zone by zone, left to right.

See Graph 6 on the left.

What it's telling you

Healthy (months 1–5): most stocks rise along with the index. It's a broad, sturdy rally.

Warning, the lines split (months 6–12): the index keeps hitting records while the typical stock quietly falls, because money is crowding into a few "safe" giants.

Leaders stall (months 12–16): the giants stop rising and nothing underneath holds the index up. It falls hard, and the typical stock falls even further.

Real examples include the "Nifty Fifty" (about 50 popular large companies) in 1972, which crashed in 1973–74, and the big tech names in 1999.

Ecommerce version: your store's revenue hits a record, but it all comes from one viral SKU while every other product declines. When that hero product cools off, there's nothing behind it.

Tech-era adjustment

Most of the market's expensiveness sits in the biggest names. Based on late-2025 forecasts for 2026 earnings, the S&P 500 traded at about 22× forward earnings and about 19× without the Magnificent 7. The typical (equal-weight) stock traded around 16.5×, roughly in line with its 10-year median. So the picture is less "the market is a bubble" and more "the biggest companies are priced for a lot of continued success."

Latest: the last 12 months

Leadership has been extremely narrow. As of late September 2026, the typical stock (equal-weight S&P 500) trailed the index by about 16% over three years, narrower breadth than in all but about 2% of months since 1929. Over 12 months it trailed by about 3%. The 10 biggest companies made up about 38% of the index, and the single largest about 8%. The twist: so far in 2026 the typical stock is slightly ahead, because several mega-cap tech names lagged. That could mean breadth is healing, or that the leaders are starting to stall. It's too early to tell which.

Act 3 · Market structure

Graph 7: Margin debt and leverage

How does borrowed money turn a small dip into a crash?

The formula

Leverage=Total position valueYour own money
Margin callwhenYour equityPosition value< 25%(FINRA minimum)

Example: $10k of your money + $40k borrowed = $50k position, so leverage = 5×. A 20% drop cuts the position to $40k, wiping out your $10k.

▲ HigherSmall drops wipe out equity and force selling. At 5× leverage, a 20% fall erases everything you put in.

▼ LowerDips stay dips, with no forced selling. Record margin debt tends to show up late in bubbles.

What it measures

Buying on margin means borrowing from your broker to buy more stock. Put in $10k, borrow $40k, and you control $50k of stock. A 10% rise then makes you 50% on your own money, but losses are magnified the same way. Margin debt tends to hit records late in a bubble, when confidence is highest.

If prices fall far enough, you get a margin call: deposit more cash, or the broker sells your stock for you. Those forced sales push prices lower, which triggers margin calls for other people and more forced selling. It becomes a chain reaction.

How to use the simulator

Every scenario starts with the same 5% drop. Drag the slider to change how much of the market runs on borrowed money, and watch what happens to that 5%.

See Graph 7 on the left.

What it's telling you

With no borrowing, a 5% dip stays a 5% dip. With heavy borrowing, forced sellers dump stock at any price, and each step on the chart is another wave of margin calls. This is a big part of why 1929 was so violent: people could buy stock with as little as 10% of their own money.

Tech-era adjustment

No adjustment needed. Margin debt is already compared against market growth here (borrowing up 49% in a year vs the market up about 21%), so it's scaled to the size of today's market.

Latest: the last 12 months

Margin debt hit a record $1.50 trillion in June 2026, up 49% in a year. That was the widest gap between borrowing growth and market growth since October 2007. July brought an $85 billion drop, the biggest monthly decline in FINRA's records, a sign that some leveraged positions were being unwound. It edged back up to about $1.45 trillion in August.

Putting it all together

Act 1, the balloon inflates (Graphs 1–3): prices get far more expensive than profits or the economy can justify.

Act 2, the pin goes in (Graphs 4–5): the central bank raises rates, borrowing gets expensive, and the yield curve inverts.

Act 3, the structure gives way (Graphs 6–7): the rally rests on a few giants, many investors are using borrowed money, and once prices slip, forced selling does the rest.

The biggest crashes had all three acts at once. None of these indicators can tell you when. CAPE was above 30 in 1997, three years before the dot-com peak, and anyone who sold then missed a huge run. These indicators measure how much risk you're taking, not when the drop will come.
